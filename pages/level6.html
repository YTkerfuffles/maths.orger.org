Statistics (It is recommended to read the technical results document once you reach the beginning of the section on characteristic functions):

To prove the central limit theorem and validity of chi squared tables, click here to see an outline of the absolute mathematical chaos you would be getting yourself into.
  • To prove that chi squared tables work, we first have to prove the central limit theorem.
  • The standard characteristic function or moment generating function argument for this theorem is actually not too hard, but it is not a valid proof. The moment generating function does not always exist. MGFs when they do exist are also unbounded so the convergence arguments used in my proof are much harder, and possibly impossible, to use. The characteristic function proof is better, but the problem is that typically it expands the characteristic function as a taylor series and then implicitly swaps expectation and an infinite sum without justifying it. There is a modified version which is a proper proof that uses a few bounding arguments though, but I have not seen this argument in the literature. And then, 99.999999999999% of proofs of CLT online, or possibly all but one proof of CLT online, by either MGF’s or CF’s, and the Cambridge part IA probability and part II probability and measure theory “proofs” state Levy’s continuity theorem without proof and use that to conclude. However, this theorem is actually about 80% of the difficulty of the proof. The characteristic function argument is the “punchline” of the proof and LCT is all technical details. The theorem is two statements in 1: That the characteristic function is injective (ie same cf implies same distribution, this is a version of the Fourier inversion theorem) and that convergence in cf actually implies convergence in distribution. The first of these can be proven by finding a formula to determine the distribution knowing its cf, but to prove that this formula is valid requires the dominated convergence theorem which requires introducing the theory of Lebesgue integration and proving some lemmas. The second of these can be proven as well using the dominated convergence theorem and some algebraic manipulation, but both of these proofs require introducing Gaussean damping to ensure absolute convergence so integrals can be swapped. The second part of the theorem also requires stuff from Real analysis like uniform continuity and even a brief cardinality argument in one place. The first part of LCT also requires a small amount of Complex analysis as the fact some of the integrals involved are path-independent in the complex plane is used. We also need to introduce the dirac delta function to give an argument for why these ideas can be applied to discrete distributions like the binomial.
  • Now once we have the Central Limit theorem, we have to prove that chi squared tables work. The simplest argument I found after searching the entire literature for days for the case where the only constraint is the total is not too hard but it uses a lot of Matrix algebra. At the end, it uses the Multivarite CLT to conclude. To prove Multivariate CLT, we need to basically do the entire Levy’s continuity theorem proof again but this time by replacing the scalars with vectors in the algebra. We also need a lemma to prove the multivariate CLT called the Cramer Wold device that essentially says that every distribution is uniquely determined by its 1D projections, which requires more of the analysis/lebesgue/dominated convergence/fourier arguments that we have had to do several times by now, as in the proof we use the converse of LCT so we have to also prove that. Loosely speaking with the Multivariate CLT we now show that the square root of the chi squared statistic converges to a multivariate normal with the right number of dimensions so the chi squared statistic has a chi squared distribution. Proving this requires introducing properties of Multivariate normals, Covariance matrices, and Projection matrices, ie we essentially need to add some undergraduate statistics background material since knowledge of this stuff is not assumed at A level. Technically, all that is used is that the distribution for the hypothesis test depends only on the degrees of freedom but the easiest way to do this as far as I know is to just prove it is a chi squared distribution with the right number of degrees of freedom.
  • This leaves the case where we have additional constraints on the chi squared table: Estimating the mean and constraining row and column totals are both linear constraints that constrain the chi squared table vector to some hyperplane. Estimating the variance does not, but this never comes up at A level so we can leave it. We note that the degrees of freedom is the dimension of the hyperplane in question and prove that we actually have a chi squared distribution with the right number of degrees of freedom. The core geometric idea is that constraining an n-dimensional normal distribution to k dimensions gives a k-dimensional normal distribution by symmetry, but some complicated bounding arguments are required to show that the actual probability of the different versions of the chi squared table play nice enough that they are smooth enough that this argument for continuous distributions can be used on the discrete approximation. After this we become done at last. For a thing used at A LEVEL. I repeat, A LEVEL. A COURSE WHICH IS SO DUMBED DOWN THAT EXAMINERS ROLL A DICE TO DECIDE HOW MANY MARKS TO DEDUCT FOR EACH SILLY MISTAKE, AND YET IT REQUIRES ALL OF THE ABOVE TO UNDERSTAND IT PROPERLY.
